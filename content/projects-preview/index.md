Our research group at the University of Iceland develops instruments and measurement techniques to investigate the early Universe and the nature of dark matter. We combine experimental microwave optics, cryogenics, electromagnetic simulations, material development, and astronomical data analysis. We tailor project involvement for new members joining the group based on their interests, experties, and desired career paths. 

## Preamble

We study cosmology, astrophysics, and fundamental physics through observations of the microwave sky. The physical processes that took place in the first epochs in the history of our Universe have left an imprint in a 2.7-Kelvin blackbody radiation field that permeates all space, known as the [cosmic microwave background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background) (CMB). In the last 60 years, by carefully observing this radiation with increasingly sensitive instruments, we have succeeded in extracting intricate cosmic detail and established a remarkably successful model of the Universe.

There are lots of great introductions to the field, here is one example: [How Ancient Light Reveals the Universe’s Contents](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-ancient-light-reveals-the-universes-contents-20200128/) 

Future experiments mapping the CMB are designed to revolutionize our understanding of the infant universe, the standard model of particle physics, structure formation, and the nature of dark matter. A key effort involves constraining or detecting the energy scale of cosmic inflation.  A detection would mark a watershed moment in cosmology and high-energy physics, but in order to push constraints significantly past current limits, our community needs to significantly improve instrument characterization capabilities. 

Motivated by these fundamental science themes, our research group is engaged in multiple experimental collaborations, including [Taurus](https://www.tauruscmb.com/), the [Simons Observatory](https://simonsobservatory.org/), the [LiteBIRD satellite](https://www.isas.jaxa.jp/en/missions/spacecraft/future/litebird.html), and a complementary programme searching for axion dark matter.

## Skuggsjá: Optical calibration and cryogenic testing

Through funding from the European Research Council, the Icelandic Research Council, and the University of Iceland (Háskóli Íslands or HÍ for short), we are setting up a new research infrastructure at HÍ. Skuggsjá is our experimental base at the University of Iceland. The lab combines an anechoic chamber, cryogenic hardware, coherent microwave instrumentation, a vector network analyzer, and six-axis robotic positioning.

The infrastructure includes various coherent microwave sources in the 40-400 GHz frequency range, a custom anechoic chamber ([see post](/post/2023_anechoic_chamber)), some robotic arms ([see arXiv:2407.04805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04805)), a vector network analyzer, and a custom cryostat that accommodates small to medium-size refractor telescopes ([see our 2026 SPIE paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19337)) and reimaging optics. This new lab is being devloped with current and future-generation CMB experiments in mind, including CMB experiments such as the balloon-borne experiment Taurus and the LiteBIRD satellite. These CMB experiments give rise to multiple projects in design and testing of novel optical components.

Read more in our [robotic beam-mapping paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04805) and these 2026 SPIE proceedings:

- Rustam Balafendiev et al., [Effects of manufacturing tolerances on the performance of metamaterial microwave anti-reflection coatings](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19176): full-wave simulations of how variations in machined surface structures affect transmission and scattering.
- Miranda Eiben et al., [Millimeter and sub-millimeter characterization of polymers used for infrared filters in high-sensitivity cryogenic microwave telescopes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18793): measurements of polymer absorption and scattering, including robot-enabled measurements from 90 to 330 GHz.
- Jón E. Guðmundsson et al., [Design, assembly, and initial test results of a cryostat for holographic characterization of microwave telescopes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19337): the design, fabrication, assembly, and room-temperature vacuum qualification of our optical-testing cryostat.

## CMBeam: cryogenic optical testing

The ERC-funded CMBeam project (2022-2028) develops techniques for characterizing microwave telescopes at cryogenic temperatures. All CMB telescopes operate at cryogenic temperatures with some optical elements cooled to only a few degrees above absolute zero. Optical measurements under representative conditions are essential as a result.

Together with Icelandic company Héðinn, we have been building a large cryostat for testing of optical components and refractor telescope assemblies. The system incorporates radiation shields at 40 K and 4 K and will support our aspirations to develop cryogenic holography.

{{< figure src="cryostat-welding.jpeg" alt="A welder works on the cryostat vacuum vessel" caption="Fabrication of the cryostat vacuum vessel at Héðinn, combining local engineering expertise with the requirements of precision microwave experiments." >}}

The project involves thermal and mechanical modelling, vacuum engineering, radiation shielding, thermometry, and integration with our optical measurement systems. Our [CryoSim work](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04613) provides a framework for studying the thermal and mechanical performance of these facilities. Our [2026 SPIE cryostat paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19337), led by Jón E. Guðmundsson, describes the hardware design, fabrication, assembly, and initial vacuum tests.

## Taurus: optics for a balloon-borne observatory

Supported by the Icelandic Research Fund, our group leads the optics design effort for [Taurus](https://www.tauruscmb.com/), a NASA-funded balloon experiment studying the polarized microwave sky. We have designed three wide-field refracting telescopes operating across multiple frequency bands. We investigate image quality, beam shape, polarization response, optical efficiency, and performance across the focal plane.

The project connects optical design and physical-optics simulations with material characterization and preparation for cryogenic validation.

For an introduction to the balloon experiment and its science, visit the [official Taurus website](https://www.tauruscmb.com/) and read:

## Simons Observatory: calibration and optical systematics

Our recent contributions to the [Simons Observatory](https://simonsobservatory.org/) focus on understanding telescope response and its impact on scientific measurements. This includes optical modelling, beam characterization, polarization effects, and frequency-dependent calibration.

A telescope’s beam changes with frequency, and its response extends beyond the bright central region. We develop models and analysis methods that connect these instrumental details to sky-map reconstruction and cosmological interpretation.

Our work spans the Small and Large Aperture Telescopes and draws on laboratory measurements, electromagnetic simulations, and collaboration-wide calibration efforts.

Xiaodong Ren et al.’s 2026 SPIE proceeding, [Physical Optics Analysis of Polarization Effects in SO LAT Reflectors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18998), examines co-polar and cross-polar beams and polarization-angle offsets across the Large Aperture Telescope’s reflector system, connecting optical modelling to polarization calibration.

## Simulations: connecting optics to sky maps

How does an imperfect telescope change our view of the microwave sky? We address this question by combining realistic optical models with simulations of astronomical observations. Some of our older work includes the development of an open-source software called [beamconv](https://github.com/AdriJD/beamconv), which simulates observations of the polarized sky using high-fidelity beam maps. These calculations help us understand how optical asymmetries, unwanted polarization response, and radiation entering away from the main beam affect scientific results. This type of work relies heavily on high-performance computing.

Two papers led by Adriaan J. Duivenvoorden introduce and extend beamconv:

- [Full-sky beam convolution for cosmic microwave background applications](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.05034) introduces the code and demonstrates how realistic beams and sidelobes affect simulated observations and reconstructed polarization.
- [Probing frequency-dependent half-wave plate systematics for CMB experiments with full-sky beam convolution simulations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.10437) extends the simulations to include non-ideal half-wave plates and their interaction with telescope beams and polarized Galactic emission.

## Broadband microwave absorbers

Microwave instruments need materials that absorb unwanted radiation before it reaches the detectors. These materials must perform across broad frequency ranges while meeting practical requirements for fabrication, installation, and cryogenic use.

We develop structured absorbers using electromagnetic simulations, material measurements, and prototype fabrication. Recent work examines how surface geometry, feature size, and material properties influence reflection, scattering, and total absorption.

{{< figure src="absorber-measurement.jpeg" alt="Microwave measurement components with a structured absorber in the background" caption="Microwave measurement hardware and a structured absorber sample. Laboratory characterization complements our numerical studies of material and component performance." >}}

A key finding is that low reflection in one direction does not necessarily imply high total absorption: radiation may instead be scattered elsewhere. This motivates our combined programme of simulations and measurements over a wider range of angles.

Our 2024 SPIE proceeding, Gaganpreet Singh et al.’s [Reflectance measurements of mm-wave absorbers using frequency-domain continuous wave THz spectroscopy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05512), describes a testbed for measuring specular and scattered reflection. It compares five absorber materials and structures over 100–500 GHz, using both polarizations and several incidence angles.

The 2026 *Applied Optics* paper led by Gaganpreet Singh, [Optimizing broadband microwave absorbers for applications in the 70–200 GHz range](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.05309), expands this work with numerical comparisons of absorber profiles, unit-cell sizes, and material properties. Some designs reach 99.5–99.9% frequency-averaged absorption at normal incidence in the simulations. The study shows that feature size relative to wavelength is particularly important and that low specular reflection alone can hide radiation scattered into other directions. Measurements of specular reflectance provide an experimental check on the simulations. These results guide the choice of absorber geometry for broadband microwave instruments. Read the [published paper](https://opg.optica.org/ao/abstract.cfm?uri=ao-65-8-2603) or our [news summary](/post/2026_absorber_paper/).

## ALPHA: searching for axion dark matter

We also participate in ALPHA, an international effort to develop a plasma haloscope for detecting axion dark matter. The experiment uses a tunable electromagnetic structure inside a strong magnetic field to search for a weak microwave signal produced by axion conversion. It combines resonator design, microwave measurements, cryogenics, and low-noise detection. Jón is a founding member and a former spokesperson of the ALPHA collaboration.
